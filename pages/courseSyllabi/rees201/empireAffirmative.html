Hereditary Empires vs. the Affirmative Action Empire

The distinction between hereditary empires and affirmative-action empires is particularly useful for understanding the Russian Empire and the Soviet Union, especially in discussions of nationalism, imperial governance, and relations between the center and the periphery.

Historian Terry Martin introduced the distiction in scholarship, particularly in his study of Soviet nationality policy, "The Affirmative Action Empire: Nations and Nationalism in the Soviet Union, 1923–1939" (2001).

1. Hereditary empires A hereditary empire is an empire in which political power and social hierarchy are based largely on inherited status, dynastic legitimacy, and established ethnic or religious privilege.

    Typical characteristics:
  • The ruling dynasty inherits political authority.
  • The dominant ethnic or religious group occupies a privileged position.
  • Subject peoples are incorporated into an imperial hierarchy rather than recognized as equal national communities.
  • The empire generally does not seek to promote the national cultures of its subject populations.
  • Assimilation into the dominant culture may be encouraged or imposed. Example: The Russian Empire

      • Example: The Russian Empire The Romanov Empire was a dynastic, hereditary empire. The Russian monarch ruled over a multinational population, but ethnic Russians and Russian Orthodox culture occupied a privileged position.

      2. Affirmative-action empires An affirmative-action empire is a multinational state that actively promotes the national identities, languages, cultures, and political representation of its non-dominant nationalities. Martin uses this term to describe the early Soviet Union, especially the period from 1923 to the 1930s.

        Its major characteristics included: Recognition of nationalities as legitimate political and cultural communities.
      • Promotion of indigenous languages in education and administration.
      • Recruitment and advancement of representatives of local nationalities into government institutions.
      • Development of national territories and autonomous republics.
      • Support for national literature, publishing, education, and cultural institutions. This policy was known as korenizatsiia (коренизация), usually translated as indigenization

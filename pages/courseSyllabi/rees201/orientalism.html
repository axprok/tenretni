How Orientalism Appears in the Film Prisoner of the Mountains

Literary and Imperial Roots: The "prisoner of the Caucasus" trope has long carried Russian imperial associations, framing the Caucasus as a frontier where Russian cultural or military power confronts a distinct, exotic "Other".

Exoticized Sounds, Images, and Landscapes: Critics and scholars note that Bodrov initially draws on familiar Orientalist visuals and sounds—such as rugged, vast mountainscapes, local traditions, and scenes of mountain dwellers gathering outdoors—that meet audience expectations of exoticism.

Subversion Through Everyday Realism: Rather than keeping Caucasian characters (Abdul-Murat, Dina, her brother (teacher) killed by Russians) as one-dimensional archetypes or pure threats, the film gradually reveals everyday normalcy, shared human routines, and moral complexity on both sides of the conflict.
